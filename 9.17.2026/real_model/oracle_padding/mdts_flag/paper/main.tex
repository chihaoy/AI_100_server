\begin{abstract}
Mixture-of-experts (MoE) language models choose each token's experts at run time, but server inference accelerators
such as Qualcomm Cloud AI 100 execute ahead-of-time (AOT) compiled programs with static shapes and no data-dependent
control flow. Each expert's capacity is therefore fixed at compile time, and because some expert in some layer receives
nearly every token of a prefill chunk, a program that never drops a token pads every expert to the chunk length; the
exported combine adds a second cost that grows with the chunk, a dense per-lane accumulator. We present \sys, which keeps
the program static and moves the decisions it cannot compile into data. Each device ranks its experts by load at run
time and binds fixed-capacity tiers to load ranks instead of expert identities, since the order statistics of per-device
load are bounded and stable across workloads while the identities are not, and a token-owned combine sums each token's
expert rows where they were computed; both need only a sort and index gathers. On Qwen3-30B-A3B across the four SoCs of
a Cloud AI 100 Ultra card, with the KV cache on device, \sys prefills 128-, 256- and 512-token chunks 4.05$\times$,
2.45$\times$ and 2.94$\times$ faster than the production compilation. With weight indirection and tile size matched,
the rank-bound capacities make the ranked program 1.13--1.27$\times$ faster; fixed-size blocks under the same compiler
are 1.26--1.71$\times$ slower; the calibrated capacities overflowed in none of 1872 evaluation chunks of eight workloads;
and a program holding several capacity profiles recovers bit-exactly from a forced overflow. We separate the reasoning that
transfers to other AOT targets from the hardware conditions under which it pays and from the compiler behavior that
shaped our implementation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) layers let a language model grow its parameter count without a matching growth in per-token
compute: a router sends each token to a few of many experts, and only those experts run
\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch}. Recent open models use MoE layers throughout; Qwen3-30B-A3B routes each
token to 8 of 128 experts in each of its 48 layers and activates 3.3B of its 30.5B parameters \citep{yang2025qwen3}, and
Mixtral and DeepSeek-V3 follow the same pattern \citep{jiang2024mixtral,deepseek2024v3}. During prefill, a serving system
processes the prompt in chunks of $T$ tokens \citep{agrawal2024sarathi}, so each MoE layer sees $8T$ token-to-expert
assignments whose distribution over experts is known only after its router runs. GPU systems absorb this variation with
kernels that accept data-dependent shapes: block-sparse GEMMs that never drop a token \citep{gale2023megablocks},
parallelism and pipelining chosen at run time \citep{hwang2023tutel}, and all-to-all exchanges sized per batch
\citep{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe}.

Server inference accelerators take a different path. Qualcomm Cloud AI 100 \citep{chatha2021qualcomm}, like
XLA-compiled TPU programs \citep{lepikhin2021gshard} and the statically scheduled Groq TSP \citep{abts2020tsp}, runs a
model as a program compiled ahead of time: tensor shapes, the partition of every operator over cores and devices, and the
schedule are fixed before the first request, and on Cloud AI 100 the compiler rejects control flow that depends on data.
Static planning is what lets the compiler orchestrate 16 cores per SoC and four SoCs per card, but for MoE it turns two
run-time quantities into compile-time constants: how many token rows each expert computes, and how expert outputs flow
back to their tokens. Both grow costly at long chunks, which prefill uses to amortize weight traffic, and they have two
structural consequences.

\textbf{(i) Padding sized by the worst chunk.} Each expert occupies a lane of fixed capacity, and every lane computes its
full capacity however few tokens it receives. Routing is skewed and workload-dependent: in our routing captures, one expert in
some layer receives 256 of 256 and 508 of 512 tokens, and the experts most loaded in one workload are rarely the most
loaded in another (\S\ref{sec:mot-pad}). A program that never drops a token therefore pads all 128 experts to
$T$, 16$\times$ the routed assignments. Calibrated per-expert capacities do not escape this: fitted to 200 prompts from
all eight workloads, they still overflow on 12--36\% of each workload's held-out prompts, and they converge on $T$.

\textbf{(ii) Reductions shaped by the compiler rather than by the tokens.} By default the partitioner slices each
expert's down projection across the four SoCs, which costs 121.6\,MiB of cross-SoC traffic per layer, and it returns
expert outputs to tokens through a dense accumulator per stage that is zero-filled, updated between stages, reduced in
serialized steps and summed across SoCs on a single SoC, spilling to DRAM from $T=256$ on. Even with the slicing removed
and the worst serialization rewritten, this combine path takes 51\%, 64\% and 68\% of an MoE layer at $T=128$, 256 and
512 in the production MXFP6 precision (\S\ref{sec:mot-red}). Expert-parallel compilation alone makes the full model 46\%
faster at $T=128$ but 11--12.5\% slower than production at 256 and 512 tokens, because the dense combine grows with $T$.

We present \sys, a design for MoE prefill that keeps the program static and moves the decisions the compiler cannot make
into data. It rests on one property that static programs share: a program cannot branch on data, but it can address by
data. On Cloud AI 100, a weight gather indexed by a run-time tensor is lowered to indirect weight DMA and runs as fast as a
static weight stream (\S\ref{sec:mot-addr}). \sys therefore fixes lanes and capacities at compile time and decides at run time which
expert each lane serves. Each SoC counts the tokens routed to its 32 experts, sorts them, and gives the expert of rank
$r$ a lane in the tier that covers $r$. Capacities bound to ranks can be small because the order statistics of per-SoC
load are stable across workloads even when expert identities are not: over 200 held-out prompts of eight workloads, the
16th most loaded expert of a SoC never received more than 12 of 128 tokens, while a fixed expert order needs up to
84--127 rows at \emph{every} position (Figure~\ref{fig:rank}). For the reduction, \sys compiles every expert whole on one SoC,
restores the on-device KV cache that this setting breaks by making attention head-parallel, and lets each SoC own its
tokens: it gathers only the rows of each token's experts that live on the SoC, sums them locally, and leaves one
elementwise cross-SoC sum that the compiler distributes as a reduce-scatter.

We implement \sys as graph transformations of the exported model and evaluate the full 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B with MXFP6
expert weights on the four SoCs of one Cloud AI 100 Ultra card, with the KV cache retained on device. \sys prefills 128-,
256- and 512-token chunks in 122, 206 and 356\,ms, 4.05$\times$, 2.45$\times$ and 2.94$\times$ faster than the
production compilation. The reduction redesign is the larger part and a prerequisite (2.06--2.38$\times$ alone). With
weight indirection and tile size matched, the rank-bound capacities make the same ranked program 1.13, 1.13 and
1.27$\times$ faster, and \sys is 1.06--1.17$\times$ faster than the best static program. Fixed-size blocks under the same
compiler and combine are 1.26--1.71$\times$ slower, because every block streams its expert's weights. Capacities calibrated
in advance overflowed in none of 1872 evaluation chunks of eight workloads, consecutive chunks through the on-device cache
are bit-identical to the capacity-$T$ program, and one program with several capacity profiles recovers bit-exactly from a
forced overflow. The error against FP32 stays at the level of the production program.

We separate three things throughout. The algorithm needs only static shapes, a sort and gathers with run-time indices.
Whether it pays depends on hardware, model and compiler: padded rows must cost time, and each device must hold many
experts relative to $k$. The Cloud AI 100 compiler shaped the implementation and much of the speedup over production
(\S\ref{sec:impl}, \S\ref{sec:transfer}).

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item Two techniques for MoE layers in static-shape programs whose reasoning does not depend on the accelerator:
  capacities bound to per-device load ranks and sized from their order statistics, and a token-owned combine whose cost
  grows with the routed rows rather than with lanes$\,\times\,T$ (\S\ref{sec:design}).
\item A realization on Cloud AI 100, with a characterization that separates what static shapes force on any AOT target
  from what this export and compiler add (\S\ref{sec:mot}, \S\ref{sec:impl}).
\item An evaluation of the full model at three chunk lengths: a step-by-step ladder, an attribution of the capacity
  series with indirection and tile size matched, a fixed-size block baseline, held-out routing from eight workloads, and
  consecutive chunks with overflow detection and recovery (\S\ref{sec:eval}); and an account of the conditions under
  which each technique pays elsewhere (\S\ref{sec:transfer}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:bg}

\subsection{MoE Inference}

An MoE layer replaces the feed-forward block with $E$ experts and a router. For a token with hidden state
$x_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the router selects the set $\mathcal{K}_t$ of $k$ experts with the largest gate values and returns
\begin{equation}
y_t=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{K}_t} w_{t,e}\,W^{\mathrm{down}}_e\big(\mathrm{SiLU}(W^{\mathrm{gate}}_e x_t)\odot W^{\mathrm{up}}_e x_t\big),
\label{eq:moe}
\end{equation}
with normalized gate weights $w_{t,e}$. Qwen3-30B-A3B uses $E=128$, $k=8$, $d=2048$ and an expert width of 768, so an
expert's three matrices hold 9\,MiB in FP16. Implementations group assignments by expert, run each expert's GEMMs over
its gathered token rows, and return the weighted rows to their tokens; under expert parallelism the experts are spread
over devices and dispatch and combine become cross-device exchanges \citep{lepikhin2021gshard,rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe}.
Training systems bound each expert's work by a capacity factor and drop the tokens beyond it
\citep{lepikhin2021gshard,fedus2022switch}; at inference, a dropped assignment changes the model's output. Prefill
processes the prompt in chunks of $T$ tokens and writes their keys and values to the KV cache; decode then generates one
token per step \citep{kwon2023vllm,agrawal2024sarathi}. A chunk gives each layer $8T$ assignments, $2T$ per SoC on average
when four SoCs hold 32 experts each.

\subsection{MoE Systems}

GPU systems handle the data-dependent shape of expert work directly: MegaBlocks computes experts as block-sparse GEMMs
and never drops tokens \citep{gale2023megablocks}, Tutel switches parallelism at run time \citep{hwang2023tutel}, and
DeepSpeed-MoE, FasterMoE and Lina optimize or schedule the all-to-all exchange
\citep{rajbhandari2022deepspeedmoe,he2022fastermoe,li2023lina}. Placement studies exploit routing structure
\citep{yao2024exflow,yu2025patterns}, \citet{huang2024toward} replace static capacity with dynamic gating, and
expert-choice routing fixes each expert's tokens by construction but changes the model \citep{zhou2022expertchoice}. On
Trainium, expert work is packed into fixed-size blocks whose weight loads are shared across an expert's blocks and whose
unused blocks are skipped \citep{awsneuronmoe}; we compare against blocks in \S\ref{sec:eval-blocks}. DynaMoE recompiles a
static-shape program when capacities change during training \citep{kossmann2022dynamoe}; our full-model programs take
6--23 minutes to compile, so capacities cannot follow each chunk by recompilation.

\subsection{AOT-Compiled Inference Accelerators}

A Cloud AI 100 SoC has 16 AI cores and 136\,GB/s of LPDDR4x bandwidth to up to 32\,GB
\citep{chatha2021qualcomm,qualcomm2025arch}; each core has a scalar VLIW processor, a vector unit, a tensor unit with 4K
FP16 MACs and 8\,MB of software-managed vector memory. The Ultra card carries four SoCs under a shared 150\,W limit.

The compiler takes an ONNX graph and one shape specialization per chunk length and produces a static program,
partitioned over the cores of a SoC and, by multi-device tensor slicing, over the SoCs. Matrix weights can be MXFP6
\citep{rouhani2023mx}, dequantized by the vector unit while the tensor unit computes. A KV cache stays on device when each
cache input can be paired with its updated output (\emph{retained state}). An ONNX \texttt{If} with a non-constant
condition is rejected; dynamic-shape compilers for CPUs and GPUs handle such workloads with run-time dispatch
\citep{shen2021nimble,zhu2021disc}, which these programs lack.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:mot}

We study the MoE layers of Qwen3-30B-A3B compiled for the four SoCs of one Cloud AI 100 Ultra card (setup in
\S\ref{sec:setup}). The exported graph \citep{qefficient} runs a layer's 128 experts as two stages of 64 \emph{lanes}; a
lane is one expert's gate, up and down GEMMs over a fixed number of gathered token rows, its capacity $C$. The production
program uses $C=T$ in both stages, the only capacity that can never drop a token. Three observations motivate \sys:
capacities attached to experts must cover the worst chunk, the compiler's combine grows with the chunk, and the compiler
nevertheless lets a program address weights by data. The first follows from static shapes and the model's routing and
holds on any AOT target; the other two describe this export and compiler.

\subsection{Capacities Attached to Experts Cover the Worst Chunk}
\label{sec:mot-pad}

Without data-dependent control flow a lane cannot size its work to its tokens: the compiler skips only whole row tiles
that hold no token (\S\ref{sec:eval-abl}), so a lane computes its tokens rounded up to the tile and, unless it is one tile,
reads all its weights. Capacity is work, and the question is which capacities a program may use without dropping tokens.

We captured the routing of all 48 layers for 50 prompts of 128 tokens from each of eight workloads, GSM8K, MMLU,
SWE-bench Lite issues, HumanEval and its JavaScript version, Alpaca instructions, CNN/DailyMail articles and Chinese XNLI
sentence pairs \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k,hendrycks2021mmlu,jimenez2024swebench,chen2021humaneval,muennighoff2024octopack,taori2023alpaca,hermann2015cnndm,conneau2018xnli},
and for chunks of 256 and 512 tokens from four of them (Appendix~\ref{app:calib}), with the model's FP32 layers on the
CPU. The load is skewed: in some layer one expert receives 127 of 128, 256 of 256 and 508 of 512 tokens. The skew is
also workload-specific: across workloads the 16 most loaded experts of a layer agree with a Jaccard index of 0.00--0.33,
against 0.52--0.88 between the two halves of one workload, and the 64 most loaded agree at 0.29--0.70 (HumanEval and its
JavaScript version, which share their problem statements, are the exception).

A program that attaches capacities to experts, fixing which lane serves which expert, must reserve at each lane the
largest load that its expert can receive. Figure~\ref{fig:rank} shows the cost. Ordering each SoC's 32 experts by their
mean load on calibration prompts and reading the held-out loads at each position of that order, the maximum is 84--127 of
128 rows at every position at $T=128$: a drop-free fixed layout needs 91\% of the rows of padding everything to $T$
(73\% at $T=512$). Fitting stage capacities to calibration data does not rescue a fixed layout: with hot and cold stages
fitted to 200 prompts from all eight workloads, 12--36\% of each workload's held-out prompts still overflow in some layer,
and the fitted capacities approach the chunk length, 122 and 105 of 128 rows in the median layer.

Padding is not free once $T$ grows. In a one-layer replay, the tensor-unit time of a SoC's 16 most loaded experts is 44,
310 and 921\,\textmu s per core at capacities 92, 158 and 296, a 21$\times$ increase for 3.2$\times$ the rows, while their
weight stream stays constant. In an instrumented profile of the full model at $T=512$, the stage that holds each SoC's 16 most
loaded experts at capacity $T$ takes a quarter of every layer (2.35 of 9.24\,ms), and even after the reduction redesign
of \S\ref{sec:red}, capacity-$T$ padding makes latency grow in proportion to the chunk (131.7, 258.9 and 498.4\,ms at
$T=128$, 256 and 512; Table~\ref{tab:ladder}), so a longer chunk gains nothing per token.

\subsection{The Compiler's Combine Grows with the Chunk}
\label{sec:mot-red}

The combine of Eq.~(\ref{eq:moe}), expert rows back to their tokens, is where the compiler's defaults cost most.

\textbf{Slicing across SoCs.} With its default heuristic the partitioner splits the down-projection weights of all 64
lanes four ways along the output dimension, so each SoC computes a 512-column slice of every expert's output. This
requires an all-gather of the intermediate activation (36\,MiB per stage at $C=128$) and a re-shard of the output
(24\,MiB per stage) before the combine; cross-SoC traffic reaches 121.6\,MiB per layer, while gate and up projections stay
expert-parallel.

\textbf{A dense accumulator.} The exported combine scatters each stage's weighted expert rows into an accumulator of
shape $[64, T, d]$, zero-filled by a single core, gathered and updated between the two stages, reduced over each SoC's
16 lanes by one einsum that the compiler runs through DRAM on one core, and finally summed over the four SoCs on four
cores of SoC~0. From $T=256$ on, this last sum also spills into a DRAM-backed reduction on one core (0.84\,ms). Every
part scales with $T$; none scales with the number of routed rows.

\subsection{A Static Program Can Address by Data}
\label{sec:mot-addr}

What the compiler withholds is control flow; what it offers is indexing. We probed three mechanisms on one-layer replays
and two-layer cuts of the full model.

\textbf{Index-gathered weights are nearly free.} Replacing the three weight constants of a layer's first stage by
\texttt{Gather(bank, idx)}, with the 64-entry index arriving at run time, leaves the layer at 2.796\,ms, the same as with
static weights, and bit-identical: the gather becomes an indirect weight DMA of the MXFP6 bytes. On the full model it
costs 0\% at $T=256$ and 6\% at 512 once tile sizes are tuned (\S\ref{sec:eval-abl}). Only this form stays fast: per-SoC
constants and batched gathers run 2.3--3.7$\times$ slower, and the one gathered constant is replicated on every SoC; one
partition per SoC stores each bank once, but the runtime then runs the SoCs one after another (1.7$\times$ slower).

\textbf{Lane counts must divide 16.} The compiler maps a stage onto a SoC's cores by its lanes per SoC: counts that divide
16 map evenly, while in our lane-split programs 17--19 lanes split the GEMMs unevenly and 20--32 lanes collapsed onto four
cores per SoC (3.7--4.3\,ms for a stage that runs in 0.5\,ms on 16 cores).

\textbf{Stages run one after another.} The compiler schedules a layer's expert stages sequentially, in its own order;
in our tiered programs a stage of $L$ lanes per SoC ran one lane per core on $L$ cores. Every additional stage adds a
window of its own.

\section{\sys}
\label{sec:design}

Section~\ref{sec:mot} leads to three requirements. First, capacity must follow each chunk's load without control flow,
so the program must choose at run time which expert a lane serves and bind capacities to a quantity that is stable across
chunks. Second, the combine must scale with the routed rows rather than with lanes$\,\times\,T$, and no device may root
it. Third, both must be static graphs built only from operations that ahead-of-time targets provide: reductions, a sort,
gathers with run-time indices and one cross-device sum. This section states the two techniques in those terms;
\S\ref{sec:impl} describes how the Cloud AI 100 compiler shaped their realization, and \S\ref{sec:transfer} separates
what transfers.

\subsection{Rank-Bound Capacities}
\label{sec:sort}\label{sec:tiers}

Each device owns a fixed set of $E_c$ experts, 32 of the 128 on each SoC here. After the router, \sys counts each
expert's tokens and sorts each device's counts, which gives a per-device permutation $\pi_c$ from rank to expert. The
device's lanes form tiers: a tier is a stage of $L_i$ lanes at capacity $C_i$ that covers ranks $a_i,\dots,a_i+L_i-1$,
with $\sum_iL_i=E_c$, and lane $j$ of the tier serves the expert of rank $a_i+j$. The lane's weights are gathered through
$\pi_c$, and its token table, the positions of the tokens it computes, is gathered from tables that the routing computes
for all experts in their fixed order. Every expert keeps exactly one lane, so tiers change only the padding: the same
experts compute the same tokens, and the output is bit-identical to any other tiering. A device computes
$\sum_iL_iC_i$ rows per layer, against $E_cT$ with capacity-$T$ padding.

\textbf{Capacities from order statistics.} The first tier has capacity $T$, since rank 1 can receive every token. For
$i>1$, $C_i$ is the largest load observed at rank $a_i$, the tier's first rank, over calibration chunks, times a headroom
$h$, rounded up to the target's row granularity and capped at $T$; since ranks are sorted, rank $a_i$ bounds the whole
tier. Order statistics are predictable for a structural reason: if a device receives $n_c$ assignments in a chunk, its
$r$-th most loaded expert holds at most $n_c/r$ of them, and $n_c$ is a sum over all of the device's experts, $2T$ on
average here and at most 1.9$\times$ that in our 256- and 512-token captures. An expert-bound capacity must instead cover
the worst case of one expert, which is $T$. Every lane's token count is computed anyway, so a chunk that overflows a tier
is detectable after the fact. We calibrate with $h=1.28$ on routing captures of the eight workloads: the 128-token prompts
of \S\ref{sec:mot-pad}, 56 chunks of 256 tokens and 20 of 512.

\textbf{Choosing the tiers.} Rows alone favor many tiers: under our calibration three tiers need 65--67\% of the rows of
a two-tier split at $T$ and $T/8$, four 52--58\% and five 48--56\%. Each tier is a stage with a fixed cost of its own, so
the number of tiers trades padded rows against per-stage cost, and the balance depends on the target
(\S\ref{sec:impl-cap}). On Cloud AI 100, \sys uses three tiers of 8, 8 and 16 lanes per SoC at capacities $T$, $C_2$
and $C_3$ of 128/48/16, 256/64/32 and 512/128/64 for $T=128$, 256 and 512: 12--13$T$ rows per SoC against $18T$ for two
tiers and $32T$ for capacity-$T$ padding.


\subsection{Token-Owned Combine}
\label{sec:red}

The combine returns each token's $k$ weighted expert rows to the token and sums them. The exported graph formulates it
per lane: each lane's rows are scattered into an accumulator of shape $[\mathrm{lanes},T,d]$, which is then reduced over
lanes, so its cost grows with lanes$\,\times\,T$ whatever the routing. \sys formulates it per token
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:combine} in Appendix~\ref{app:alg}). The router's top-$k$ output already lists each token's experts; with integer arithmetic on
the lane layout and the per-expert slot tables that the routing computes anyway, every (token, $j$) pair maps to the tier
and row of the device's compact expert outputs that hold its result, or to a masked entry when the expert lives on another
device. One gather per tier fetches at most $k$ rows per token, and a sum over them gives the device's $[T,d]$ partial
output. Each core owns a tile of tokens, so the per-device combine needs no cross-core traffic, and one elementwise sum
over the devices' partials completes the layer. The cost grows with $kT$ rather than with the lanes per device times $T$,
and when the last sum is split by token tiles, no device receives all partials.

\section{Realization on Cloud AI 100}
\label{sec:impl}

The techniques of \S\ref{sec:design} need only generic operations; how we realized them was shaped by the Cloud AI 100
compiler at each step. This section records those choices, and \S\ref{sec:transfer} summarizes which of them would change
on another target.

\subsection{Expert-Parallel Compilation with an On-Device KV Cache}
\label{sec:ep}

We compile with a weight-splitting degree of one across SoCs (\texttt{-mdts-mos=1}), which forbids cutting any weight
matrix across SoCs. The lane axis is then the only way to distribute the batched expert GEMMs, and each SoC computes 16
whole experts per stage, one per core; the all-gather and re-shard of the down projection disappear, and the layer's
cross-SoC traffic falls from 121.6 to 1.5\,MiB.

The setting is global and also stops the compiler from splitting the attention projections by KV head. The cache update
then comes out token-sliced while its input is whole, the compiler cannot pair the 96 cache inputs with their outputs,
and all 192 KV buffers become host I/O. We restore the pairing at the graph level with \emph{head-parallel attention}:
the four KV-head groups of eight query heads each \citep{ainslie2023gqa} become a leading batch axis of every attention
operator, with per-group weights of shapes $[4,2048,1024]$ for queries, $[4,2048,128]$ for keys and values and
$[4,1024,2048]$ for the output projection, whose four partial outputs are summed at the end. Under expert-parallel
compilation the batch axis lands one group per SoC, the cache operations of KV head $h$ stay on SoC $h$, and the program
again exposes no KV buffer; the regrouped output projection changes only the FP16 summation order.

\subsection{Lowering the Combine}
\label{sec:impl-comb}

\textbf{Dense-path rewrites.} Before the combine is replaced, three rewrites remove serialized work from the exported one.
(i) The first stage's outputs were added to rows gathered from the freshly zeroed accumulator; we drop this read of
zeros. (ii) The per-SoC sum over lanes, one einsum that the compiler ran through DRAM on one core, is cut into 16 tiles of
$T/16$ tokens. (iii) The routing's prefix sums over tokens, which give each assignment its row in the expert's compact
input and ran as a serial \texttt{CumSum} on one core, become Hillis--Steele scans of $\log_2 T$ shift-and-add steps
\citep{hillis1986data}. All three leave the output bit-identical. They repair this export and this compiler's lowering
rather than change the combine's formulation, and the evaluation keeps them as a separate step.

\textbf{Token-owned combine.} The device gather does not zero-fill out-of-range indices, so a safe index and a mask are
both required, and the per-SoC work must stay on the lane axis the compiler already splits over SoCs: written as explicit
per-SoC slices, the combine shipped expert outputs between SoCs (3 to 78\,MiB) and doubled the layer's device time.

\textbf{Elementwise final sum.} Written as 16 einsum tiles, the sum over the four partials ran on four cores per SoC;
written as the elementwise tree $(P_0+P_1)+(P_2+P_3)$ per token tile, it spreads over all cores, and the compiler lowers it
as a reduce-scatter that the next layer's input normalization completes.

\subsection{Lowering the Rank Sort and the Tiers}
\label{sec:impl-cap}

\textbf{One gathered constant.} The counts come from the routing mask, and the per-SoC sort is a \texttt{TopK} over each
SoC's 32 counts. Each tier's three weight matrices come from \texttt{Gather(bank,$\,\pi$)} on one constant that holds all
128 experts, the only gather form this compiler keeps fast, lowered to indirect DMA (\S\ref{sec:mot-addr}). The constant
is replicated, so every SoC holds the weights of all 128 experts, 22.5\,GiB per SoC instead of 6.7\,GiB, within the SoC's
32\,GB; the algorithm itself needs only each SoC's own 32 experts.

\textbf{Off the critical path.} The routing mask, the prefix scans and the slot tables are computed once over all 128
experts in native order, independent of the sort; only a row permutation of these tables and the weight gathers wait for
it. Sorting first and running the routing on permuted columns makes everything wait (12 layers: +2.5\,ms over
capacity-$T$ padding, against +1.6\,ms). Gathered weights are also sensitive to the compiler's maximum tile size: with the
default, the first expert GEMM waits 0.085\,ms for its first gathered tile, with 512\,KiB tiles 0.032\,ms. For the rank sort the best
size is 512\,KiB for $T\le256$ and 1024\,KiB for $T=512$ (Appendix~\ref{app:tiles}); other programs differ (Table~\ref{tab:attr}).

\textbf{Tier widths and count.} Stages map evenly only when their lanes per SoC divide 16, run one after another, and
use $L$ cores for $L$ lanes (\S\ref{sec:mot-addr}), so tiers take widths from $\{1,2,4,8,16\}$. At $T=512$ the first tier
is bound by the SoC's shared memory traffic: eight lanes of 512 rows finish in 0.8--1.0\,ms, sixteen in 2.2\,ms. Three
tiers of 8, 8 and 16 lanes balance these effects (\S\ref{sec:eval-pad}).

\subsection{Graph Transformations}
\label{sec:impl-graph}

\sys is a set of ONNX graph transformations, about 1,100 lines of Python, applied to the Qwen3-30B-A3B graph that the
vendor's QEfficient library exports for Cloud AI 100 \citep{qefficient}. Each step of the ladder (\S\ref{sec:setup}) is a
separate pass, so every intermediate program can be compiled and measured. The passes locate operators by structure rather than
by name (layer 0 numbers some nodes differently and folds a 64-lane constant into its stage), regenerate the
$T$-dependent constants of the scans and tiles for each chunk length, and clone the stage subgraph once per tier with its
own row order, weight gathers and capacity. A static \texttt{If} in the export that squeezes the token table is replaced
by the equivalent \texttt{Squeeze} in every clone, and the combine's map from a lane to (tier, SoC, row) becomes a set of
small per-tier tables. The same pass builds the evaluation's variants: the export's own combine, fixed expert order
through the gathers, fixed-size blocks, and tier capacities read from input lengths for the overflow fallback.

Each chunk length is its own program, with sequence length $T$ and context $2T$, MXFP6 matrix weights, FP16
activations, 16 cores per SoC, depth-first scheduling, expert-parallel slicing and retained state
(Appendix~\ref{app:flags}). Static programs occupy 25\,GiB, programs with gathered weights 88\,GiB. Every program returns each lane's token count in every layer.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We ask how much \sys speeds up the full model (\S\ref{sec:eval-e2e}), what the capacities contribute once indirection
and tiling are matched (\S\ref{sec:eval-abl}), what each part of the reduction and of the padding design contributes
(\S\ref{sec:eval-red}, \S\ref{sec:eval-pad}), how ranked lanes compare with fixed-size blocks (\S\ref{sec:eval-blocks}),
whether the programs hold beyond the first chunk and under overflow (\S\ref{sec:eval-chunks}), whether the calibration
generalizes (\S\ref{sec:eval-gen}), and whether the output is preserved (\S\ref{sec:eval-acc}).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Hardware and model.} One Cloud AI 100 Ultra card, four SoCs with 16 cores and 32\,GB each, platform SDK 1.21.6.
Qwen3-30B-A3B, all 48 layers, batch 1, MXFP6 matrix weights and FP16 activations as in the production deployment. Every
program keeps its KV cache on device.

\textbf{Programs.} Seven programs, each adding one step to the one before it (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}): (1) the production
compilation of the exported graph, with default partitioning, the dense combine and capacity $T$ in both stages;
(2)~expert-parallel compilation with head-parallel attention, which keeps the export's combine and capacity $T$ and is
our \emph{naive \naive} baseline; (3) the dense-path rewrites; (4) the token-owned combine; (5) the elementwise final sum;
(6) the run-time rank sort with two tiers at $T$ and $T/8$; (7) \sys's three tiers.

\textbf{Prompts.} At $T=128$ the first chunk of a GSM8K chat prompt; at 256 and 512 held-out chunks built from GSM8K
test questions outside the calibration range. Latency depends on a chunk only through its routing, and on a single layer
it varied by about 1\% across 200 prompts of four workloads.

\textbf{Protocol.} Host latency of one chunk, the median of three rounds of 20 inferences after warm-up. All programs of
one chunk length run back to back in one session, with anchor programs repeated before and after; steps 6 and 7 ran in a
second session and are chained to the first through step 5, which ran in both and agreed within 0.13\%. Rounds agree
within 0.6\%. The attribution programs of \S\ref{sec:eval-abl} ran in sessions of two or three, each timed twice in mirrored order,
every session holding an anchor; a program$\,+\,$session fit of log latency combines them (largest residual 0.34\%). For accuracy we compare the last-position logits with an FP32 CPU run of the same chunk, check the
next-token prediction, and read back every lane's token count.

\textbf{Why the tile size matters.} The compiler cuts each lane's rows into tiles and runs a tile only if a run-time
predicate, derived from the export's mask of empty slots, finds a token in it; an empty expert's weights are skipped only
when its lane is one tile (Appendix~\ref{app:attrprof}). The tile size sets the tiles: a 512-row lane is one tile at the

the prerequisite.

\subsection{Reduction}
\label{sec:eval-red}

\textbf{Expert parallelism alone is not enough.} Step 2 removes the down projection's slicing and is 46\% faster than
production at $T=128$. At 256 and 512 tokens it is 12.5\% and 11.0\% slower, because the dense
combine that remains grows with $T$ (Table~\ref{tab:combine}); the head-parallel attention in this step only restores the
on-device cache.

\textbf{Representation as well as lowering.} The dense-path rewrites save 37--41\% at every chunk length by repairing
the export's lowering of the dense combine; replacing the dense representation by the token-owned combine saves another
16.9\%, 27.3\% and 31.6\%, growing with $T$ as the accumulator it removes does. In instrumented two-layer profiles at
$T=512$ it cuts the time after the last expert GEMM from 4.7--5.5 to 1.2--1.4\,ms per layer and the MoE's cross-SoC traffic
from 48 to 14\,MiB. The elementwise final sum adds 1.5--2.6\%.


\subsection{Padding}
\label{sec:eval-pad}

\textbf{The sort is cheap; the capacities pay.} Step 6 of the ladder saves 2.5\%, 14.6\% and 14.2\%, but it also
changes weight access and tile size. With both matched (Table~\ref{tab:attr}) the sort itself costs 0--2\% at $T=256$ and
512 but 9\% at 128, where it sits on a short layer's critical path (B$\to$C), and the two tiers give 1.09--1.11$\times$ (C$\to$D). The sort adds a dependency on every layer's
critical path, router to sort to weight gathers, which the tile sizes of \S\ref{sec:impl-cap} keep short.



\textbf{Three tiers, not more.} Step 7 saves another 3.2\%, 4.2\% and 15.5\%. At $T=512$ the 16 lanes at capacity 512
take 2.2\,ms per layer; as eight lanes at 512 and eight at 128, the same ranks take 0.8--1.0 and 0.65--0.72\,ms
(Figure~\ref{fig:stages}, Appendix~\ref{app:stages}), since the stage is bound by the SoC's memory traffic. A fourth tier
removes more rows but adds a sequential window: on two layers, four tiers are 8.6\% faster than two, three tiers 14.1\%.
At $T=256$ the extra window absorbs most of the saving.

\subsection{Fixed-Size Blocks Instead of Ranked Lanes}
\label{sec:eval-blocks}

Block scheduling packs assignments into blocks of $B$ rows, one expert per block, and provisions
$\lceil kT/B\rceil + E - 1$ blocks to drop nothing \citep{awsneuronmoe}. We built it from \sys's own stage body, routing,
gathers and combine: each SoC computes in the graph the blocks per expert, the expert and rows of every block slot, and
the combine reads an assignment at row $M_e B + s$. Budgets per SoC: dropless for the worst local load of $8T$
assignments, $\min(\lceil 8T/B\rceil + 31, 32\lceil T/B\rceil)$; calibrated by \sys's rule; and, as a non-deployable
diagnostic, the timed prompt's active blocks. Outputs are bit-identical to \sys.



The first block programs were 2.0--4.8$\times$ slower because the reused stage body materialized the hidden states once per
lane (192\,MiB per SoC and layer at 96 lanes); a plain gather of the rows removed that (Table~\ref{tab:blocks} in Appendix~\ref{app:blocks}) but does
not help \sys (16.4 against 16.0\,ms). What remains is weight traffic: every block slot streams its expert's MXFP6 weights (3.52\,MiB), used or
not, so the 64-row dropless program reads 337.5\,MiB per SoC and layer against 95--106\,MiB for \sys, whose empty experts
skip their reads. One weight load cannot serve consecutive blocks of an expert in this compiler; merging them into one
lane with a static row count is a capacity, i.e.\ \sys. At $T=512$ the best calibrated block program is 1.26$\times$ slower than
\sys, the best dropless one 1.51$\times$, and even the trace-specialized one 1.12$\times$, as its single stage runs four
lanes per core on 12 of the 16 cores and spills; at $T=256$ these are 1.39, 1.59 and 1.13$\times$, at 128 1.69, 1.71 and 1.31$\times$. Blocks
lose on weight traffic, not rows: at $T=512$ the trace-specialized programs and the calibrated ones with $B\le64$ provision fewer rows than \sys.

\subsection{Beyond the First Chunk, and Overflow}
\label{sec:eval-chunks}

The programs keep the cache for $2T$ positions. A second chunk attends to the cache the first wrote: \sys and the static
capacity-$T$ program give bit-identical logits for both chunks at $T=128$, 256 and 512, matching the FP32 reference's top
five at positions $T-1$ and $2T-1$ and its next token except at two near-ties at $T=128$ (0.38 and 0.58 logits apart),
and the second chunk costs what the first does.

The lower tiers are calibrated, not dropless, so a deployment must detect overflow and recover. One program can hold
several capacity profiles: tier capacities come from the lengths of two small row inputs, and the compiler specializes the
program per profile over the same weights and on-device cache. After every chunk the host compares each lane's routed count
with its capacity; on overflow it discards the outputs and reruns the chunk with the capacity-$T$ profile at the same
positions, which rewrites every cache entry the failed attempt wrote. With an undersized profile forced on the second of
four chunks of the full model, the overflow was detected in 45 (layer, lane) pairs from layer 0 on, the rejected logits were 0.04 relative
$L_2$ off, and after the rerun all four chunks were bit-identical to runs without overflow, at full capacity and on the
static program; the same holds for an overflow in the first chunk. Capacity inputs and extra profiles make the tiered
program 18\% slower, however, so a static capacity-$T$ program kept resident beside \sys for replays may be the cheaper
fallback.

\subsection{Do Calibrated Capacities Generalize?}
\label{sec:eval-gen}

We split each of eight workloads into calibration and evaluation documents before cutting chunks and measured the routing
of every chunk on the device. Calibrated without headroom, the rank rule reproduces \sys's capacities exactly at $T=256$
(at 512 it gives a 112-row middle tier where \sys uses 128, which overflows in 2 of 449 chunks; at 128 a 32-row one where
\sys uses 48). \sys's capacities overflowed in none of the 805, 618 and 449 evaluation chunks at $T=128$, 256 and 512 and in
no workload (30 chunks hold documents they were calibrated on), nor did leave-one-workload-out calibration. The same tiers bound to a fixed expert order need capacity $T$ everywhere, and even
calibrated per workload they need 1.4--2.7$\times$ the rows and still overflow in up to 30\% of chunks: at $T=512$ the
busiest expert at a SoC's positions 9--16 or 17--32 exceeds 128 tokens in 30 or 23\% of (chunk, layer, SoC) cases, because which experts are
busy moves from chunk to chunk, while ranks 1, 9 and 17 never exceeded 509, 119 and 52 tokens. The bound $n_{(r)}\le N/r$
holds with room (at most 0.97 of it).

\subsection{Accuracy}
\label{sec:eval-acc}



Every program stays at the error level of the production program's MXFP6 weights: the relative $L_2$ error of the
last-position logits against FP32 is 0.170, 0.087 and 0.088 for \sys at $T=128$, 256 and 512, against 0.188, 0.089 and
0.095 for production (Appendix~\ref{app:acc}). The programs differ from production only in FP16 summation order
(head-parallel attention regroups the output projection; the token-owned combine reorders a token's rows), and the
attribution and block programs are bit-identical to \sys. The next token matches FP32 except at 256 tokens, whose two
leading FP32 logits are 0.58 apart. No lane exceeded its capacity.



\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Scope.} The lower tiers are calibrated, not dropless: no evaluation chunk overflowed, but the deployed rate depends
on model and workload. One model at batch 1; two consecutive chunks at every $T$ and four at $T{=}128$, but no decode,
which needs a decode graph that our $T$-specific rewrites do not provide; blocks under this compiler only. The SoCs share a
150\,W limit under which most padded programs throttle at times (\sys never does), so we compare programs within a session
or across sessions through repeated anchors.

\section{What Transfers}
\label{sec:transfer}

The speedups of \S\ref{sec:eval} combine an algorithm, conditions of hardware and model that decide how much it pays,
and compiler behavior that decided how we built it; we call a part transferable when the same reasoning applies on another
target, even if the transformation and the speedup differ (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}).



\textbf{The algorithm.} Capacities fixed before routing is known should follow load ranks, the combine should be owned
by tokens, and it must be fixed before the padding (\S\ref{sec:eval-abl}). All three need only static shapes, a sort and
gathers with run-time indices. The order-statistics bound holds for any routing; its stability across workloads is a
property of the model, measured here for one.

\textbf{When it pays.} The techniques target programs that fix an expert's work before routing is known; kernels that
size expert work at run time have no padding to remove \citep{gale2023megablocks}. Padded rows must cost time, which
depends on the hardware and on the compiler: a 16-lane stage of ours streams its MXFP6 weights in about 0.6\,ms, stays at
that floor with 92 rows per lane and is bound by its tensor-unit time with 296, and the compiler skips padded rows only in
whole tiles, coarser ones for gathered weights, so the capacities gain 1.13--1.27$\times$ over the ranked program
and 1.06--1.17$\times$ over the best static one. Blocks need what whole-expert lanes do not, a weight load shared by an
expert's consecutive blocks or skipped for unused ones; where a stack provides both \citep{awsneuronmoe} they may close
the 1.12--1.71$\times$ gap we measured. Both techniques need many experts per device relative to $k$ (32 per SoC against
$k=8$ here; 8 experts with $k=2$ on four devices would leave little to rank), gathers that cost about as much as a static
weight stream, and, for the combine, a lane-dense baseline such as this export's or GShard's einsums
\citep{lepikhin2021gshard}.

\textbf{What the compiler shaped.} The dense-path rewrites (1.60--1.69$\times$) repair this export and compiler; the
token-owned combine and the elementwise final sum add 1.22--1.48$\times$ after them. The production baseline reflects the
default partitioning: 1.85$\times$ slower than naive \naive at $T=128$ but 1.11--1.13$\times$ faster at 256 and 512, so our
speedups over production do not transfer, while the reasoning does. A scheduler that overlaps independent stages would
favor four or five tiers.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Ahead-of-time compilation fixes how many rows each expert computes and how its outputs return to their tokens before
routing is known. \sys moves both decisions into data: capacities follow per-device load ranks, whose order statistics are
bounded and stable when expert identities are not, and the combine is owned by tokens. On Qwen3-30B-A3B across four Cloud
AI 100 SoCs, \sys prefills 128-, 256- and 512-token chunks 4.05$\times$, 2.45$\times$ and 2.94$\times$ faster than the
production compilation; with indirection and tiling matched, its capacities give 1.13--1.27$\times$ and beat fixed-size
blocks by 1.26--1.71$\times$.

\textbf{Tile predication.} The export ends each expert lane with $\mathrm{Where}(r<n_e,\,y,\,0)$, where $n_e$ is the
expert's run-time token count. The compiler lowers it as a range select that writes the zero rows directly and gates the
work producing the others: it cuts the lane's rows into tiles, evaluates for each tile at run time whether it holds a row
below $n_e$ (a compare and a reduction to a scalar), and runs the tile's activation gather, GEMMs and SiLU only if so. A lane
with $n$ tokens and row tile $R$ therefore computes $R\lceil n/R\rceil$ rows, which holds exactly for every lane of every
stage and SoC of the programs above: a 512-row lane is one tile at the default ($R=512$), two at 1024\,KiB and four at
512\,KiB, and a lane spread over two cores is cut per core (\sys's 128-row lanes: $R=64$). Per SoC, A computes 13.8--15.4k
of its 16.4k rows at the default and 3.5--3.8k at 512\,KiB, B and C 6.9--7.7k at 1024\,KiB, and \sys 3.3--3.5k of 6.1k.
An expert's weight load and dequantization feed all of its lane's tiles: for an empty expert they are skipped only when the
lane is one tile (A at the default, the 64-row tiers of D and \sys) and run otherwise. At 512\,KiB the gathered program C
computes 128-row tiles like A but spills the activations of its GEMMs to DRAM (95--97\,MiB written and read back per SoC,
against none for A), which lengthens its stages. Both rules are read off the traces; the compiler does not document them.

\section{The Token-Owned Combine}
\label{app:alg}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Token-owned combine on SoC $c$}
\label{alg:combine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE top-$k$ experts $\mathcal{K}[t,1..k]$ of each token $t$; lane map $\ell(e)\mapsto(s,c',l)$ (stage or tier, SoC, lane);
  $\mathrm{slot}[e,t]$, row of token $t$ in expert $e$'s input; weighted outputs $O_s\in\mathbb{R}^{L_sC_s\times d}$ of SoC $c$
\FOR{all $t\le T$, $j\le k$ \textbf{in parallel}}
  \STATE $e\leftarrow\mathcal{K}[t,j]$;\; $(s,c',l)\leftarrow\ell(e)$
  \STATE $\mathit{idx}_{s'}[t,j]\leftarrow0$;\; $m_{s'}[t,j]\leftarrow0$ for every $s'$ \COMMENT{masked}
  \IF{$c'=c$}
    \STATE $\mathit{idx}_s[t,j]\leftarrow l\,C_s+\mathrm{slot}[e,t]$;\; $m_s[t,j]\leftarrow1$
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each core $q$, owning tokens $t\in$ tile $q$}
  \STATE $P_c[t]\leftarrow\sum_s\sum_{j}m_s[t,j]\,O_s[\mathit{idx}_s[t,j]]$ \COMMENT{$\le k$ rows}
\ENDFOR
\STATE $y\leftarrow(P_0+P_1)+(P_2+P_3)$ \COMMENT{elementwise, over SoCs}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
